Credit cards, explained simply

A credit card can give you free short-term credit, rewards and a better credit score, or a costly debt trap. The difference is a few simple rules. Here they are, in plain words with ₹ examples.

What a credit card actually is

A credit card lets you spend the bank's money now and pay it back later. Every swipe, tap or online payment is a small, short-term loan.

Debit card

Spends your own money, straight from your bank account. If the balance is ₹0, the payment fails.

Credit card

Spends the bank's money, up to a limit. You get a bill every month and must pay it back.

Who issues it

Banks and some finance companies (NBFCs), usually on a network like Visa, Mastercard, RuPay or Amex. The issuer sets your limit, fees and interest.

The deal

Pay the full bill on time and the loan costs you nothing. Pay late or only partly and it gets expensive, fast.

Credit limit and utilisation

Your credit limit is the most the bank will let you owe at one time. If your limit is ₹1,00,000 and you've spent ₹40,000, you can spend only ₹60,000 more until you pay.

Credit utilisation is how much of your limit you're using. Credit bureaus watch it. Using a big chunk of your limit every month can look like you depend on credit, and can pull your score down.

Tip: try to keep usage under about 30% of your limit. For a ₹50,000 limit, that's ₹15,000. If you spend more, paying part of it before the statement date lowers the amount that gets reported.

The cycle in one picture

A card runs in repeating cycles. The bank adds up everything you spent in a cycle, issues a statement, and gives you a few weeks to pay it. Every purchase is an interest-free loan for a limited time, and how long depends on when you spend relative to your statement date.

Statement date

The day the bank closes the cycle and totals your spending. Purchases after this day go into the next statement.

Due date

The last day to pay without interest. It usually falls 15 to 20 days after the statement date, and your bank sets it.

Total amount due

The full statement balance. Paying this by the due date means zero interest on those purchases.

Minimum amount due

A small slice (often about 5%). Paying only this avoids a late fee but starts interest on the rest, commonly 3.5% a month or more.

Example: one card, two purchase days

Take a card with a statement on the 10th and a due date 20 days later (the 30th).

Buy on the 11th, the day after the statement

It lands in the next statement (10th of next month), due the 30th of next month. You get about 50 days before paying. This is the best case.

Buy on the 9th, the day before the statement

It lands in this month's statement, due the 30th. You get only about 21 days. This is the worst case.

Rule of thumb: spend on the card whose statement date just passed, and your payment is pushed as far away as possible.

How interest really works

If you pay the total amount due by the due date, you pay no interest. If you pay anything less, even ₹1 less, three things happen:

1. Interest from the purchase date

Interest is charged on the unpaid amount, counted from the day you made each purchase, not from the due date. Rates are often about 3% to 3.75% a month, roughly 36% to 45% a year.

2. You lose the free days

Until you clear the balance in full, new purchases also start charging interest from day one. The 20 to 50 interest-free days disappear.

3. Plus 18% GST

GST of 18% is added on top of the interest. So 3.5% a month really costs you about 4.1%.

Worked example (approximate)

Your statement shows ₹20,000 total due and ₹1,000 minimum due. You pay only ₹1,000. Interest rate: 3.5% a month.

  • ₹19,000 stays unpaid and keeps collecting interest.
  • Because interest runs from each purchase date, the next statement often shows about ₹700 to ₹900 in interest, plus about ₹130 to ₹160 GST on it.
  • Anything new you buy this month also gets charged interest from the day you buy it.
  • Keep paying only the minimum, and clearing that ₹20,000 can take years and cost about ₹— in interest and GST (see the estimate below).

Fees and charges

Interest is not the only cost. These are common charges in India. Amounts vary a lot by bank and card, so check your card's schedule of charges.

Joining and annual fee

From ₹0 to a few thousand rupees a year. Often waived if you spend above a set amount in a year.

Late payment fee

Usually a slab based on how much you owe, often a few hundred to over ₹1,000. Under RBI rules, the late fee and interest apply only if you're more than 3 days past the due date. Still, pay on time: don't treat those days as extra free time.

Cash withdrawal

A fee, often about 2.5% to 3.5% of the amount (with a minimum), plus interest from day one. No free days at all. Avoid it.

Foreign currency markup

About 2% to 3.5% on purchases in other currencies, including many international websites, plus GST.

Over-limit fee

If you go past your limit (only possible if you've opted in), a fee is often charged. You can keep this facility switched off.

Fuel surcharge

About 1% at petrol pumps. Some cards refund it within a spending range.

GST on everything

18% GST is added to fees and interest. A ₹500 fee really costs ₹590.

Others to watch

EMI processing fees, card replacement, reward redemption fees, and charges on rent or wallet loads on some cards.

Your credit score (CIBIL and others)

A credit score is a number from 300 to 900 that shows how reliably you repay. Credit bureaus in India (CIBIL, Experian, Equifax, CRIF High Mark) build it from your loan and card history. A score of about 750 or more is generally seen as good.

Helps your score

  • Paying every bill on time, every month
  • Keeping utilisation low (under about 30%)
  • Keeping older cards open: a longer history helps

Hurts your score

  • Late or missed payments
  • Using most of your limit
  • Applying for many cards or loans in a short time
  • Settling a card for less than you owe

Why it matters

Banks check it for home, car, personal and education loans. A good score can mean faster approval, a lower interest rate and higher limits.

Rewards, cashback and EMI

Reward points

You earn points per ₹ spent, often a small percentage of value. Redeem them for vouchers, flights or statement credit. Points can expire, and some categories (fuel, rent, wallets) may earn nothing.

Cashback

A percentage of your spend comes back as credit on your statement, often with monthly caps. Simple and easy to value.

"No-cost" EMI

Split a big purchase into monthly payments. The interest is usually offset by a discount from the seller, but a processing fee + 18% GST often still applies, and you may lose an upfront cash discount.

EMI blocks your limit

Convert a ₹60,000 phone to EMI and ₹60,000 of your limit is blocked. It frees up slowly as you pay each instalment, which also raises your utilisation.

Rewards are worth a few percent. Interest is worth about 40% a year. Never carry a balance to chase points.

Stay safe

  • Never share your OTP, CVV or PIN with anyone, including people claiming to be from your bank. Banks never ask for them.
  • In your bank app, switch off international, online or contactless use when you don't need it. Turn it on only when needed.
  • Set transaction limits for online, contactless and ATM use.
  • Lost card or strange charge? Block the card immediately in the app or by calling the bank. Reporting quickly (ideally within 3 days) usually limits your liability.
  • Read every statement and SMS alert. Dispute charges you don't recognise.
  • Pay only on trusted sites and apps. Don't save card details on sites you rarely use.
  • For cyber fraud, you can also call the national helpline 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in.

Is a credit card right for you?

Good fit if you…

  • Have steady income and can pay the full bill every month
  • Track your spending and like reminders and auto-pay
  • Want to build a credit score for future loans
  • Want rewards, purchase protection or an emergency backup

Avoid it (for now) if you…

  • Often run short before payday
  • Already struggle to pay loans or bills
  • Tend to overspend when money feels "free"
  • Plan to use it for cash withdrawals or to carry a balance

Habits that keep it free

  • Pay the total amount due, not the minimum. Set up auto-debit for the full amount.
  • Set one reminder per card, 3 days before each due date: the 18th, 27th and 2nd for your cards.
  • Pay a day early. Bank transfers over a weekend or holiday can post late.
  • Cash withdrawals have no grace period. Interest starts the same day.
  • Keep usage under about 30% of your limit. Pay early in the cycle if you spend more.
  • Only spend what's already in your bank account. Treat the card like a debit card that pays later.
  • Check your statement and credit report a few times a year. Checking your own score doesn't lower it.
  • Don't apply for many cards at once. Space applications by a few months.
  • Read the fee schedule before you accept a new card or an EMI offer.
  • Interest-free days only apply if the previous statement was paid in full.

FAQ

Does checking my own credit score lower it?

No. Checking your own score is a "soft" check and doesn't affect it. Only "hard" checks, when you apply for a loan or card, can lower it a little.

What if I miss the due date by a day?

Pay right away. Under RBI rules, late fees and interest apply only if you're more than 3 days past due, and it shouldn't be reported as late within that window. Paying within that window usually avoids both. Still, don't rely on it: set up auto-pay and reminders.

Should I close my old card?

Usually not, if it has no or low annual fee. An old card adds to your credit history and total limit, which keeps utilisation low. Close it if the fee isn't worth it, after paying it off fully.

Is paying the minimum due enough?

It keeps you from being marked late, but interest of about 3–3.75% a month (plus GST) starts on the rest, and new purchases lose their free days. Pay the total whenever you can.

How many credit cards should I have?

One or two is enough for most people. More cards can help with timing and rewards, but only if you can track every due date.

Can I pay before the statement date?

Yes. Paying early frees up your limit and lowers the balance that gets reported, which helps utilisation. You'll still get a statement for anything left.
